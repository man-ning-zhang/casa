My research begins with a dissertation on how obesity is governed in China and expands into a comparative program on how obesity becomes a site of pharmaceutical governance, across both patient experience and public payment. A parallel stream studies the everyday social life of fitness.

<div class="entry-card">
<p class="entry-card__year">Dissertation &middot; developing into a book project</p>
<h3 class="entry-card__title">What Became of the Movement? The Politics of Obesity and Weight Control in China</h3>
<div class="entry-card__body">
<p>My dissertation asks how obesity is governed in contemporary China and how particular forms of knowledge become authoritative. Around 2024, the Chinese state launched a high-profile response centred on physical activity and non-pharmacological health maintenance. Yet my fieldwork found its everyday effects more limited than policy documents suggest, while obesity was increasingly framed as a chronic disease requiring medical treatment, particularly amid the rapid expansion of GLP-1 therapies. This tension between state-led lifestyle intervention and growing biomedical and pharmaceutical authority provides the dissertation's central puzzle.</p>
<p>In 2026 I conducted fieldwork in Shanghai and Beijing, combining participant observation with over 50 interviews across medical, pharmaceutical, fitness, and patient communities, supplemented by policy, media, clinical guideline, and social-media analysis. My findings show that obesity is governed through competition and coordination among multiple forms of expertise, with the state actively <strong>brokering</strong> their authority by selecting, endorsing, standardising, and sometimes resisting particular claims. Non-state actors therefore seek not only professional legitimacy but also institutional relationships through which their knowledge can become authoritative, while patients draw on embodied and online knowledge to negotiate expert advice.</p>

<h3 class="entry-card__title">The Pharmaceuticalization of Obesity Medicine: A Comparative Perspective</h3>
<div class="entry-card__body">
<p>This comparative research program asks how obesity becomes governable through pharmaceutical means, and how states, firms, and patients broker the knowledge and money that make drug treatment legitimate and payable. It proceeds along two lines.</p>

<h4 class="entry-card__subtitle"><span class="idx">1.</span>Patient Voice in the United States and China</h4>
<p>When do patient claims about a contested condition become politically consequential, and when are they instead absorbed into the commercial and institutional infrastructures that govern care? Patient voice is often described as a democratic corrective to expert authority, yet the conditions under which it actually reshapes governance remain poorly specified. This project compares obesity patient activism in China and the United States, asking what happens when patients try to turn the experience of living with a condition into a claim on the state.</p>
<p>Political regimes shape how patients voice their demands, but democratic and authoritarian contexts yield a shared outcome. In the United States, advocacy runs through institutional avenues such as lobbying and litigation. In China, where patients lack formal channels, collective action flourishes in informal digital spaces and corporate-backed networks. Pharmaceutical companies build dedicated patient-advocacy divisions, frequently working through philanthropic foundations as intermediaries. Patient activism is then redirected away from collective critique toward individual treatment choices and market expansion, through staged storytelling, charity walks, and sponsored workshops.</p>
<p>To assess whether this amplified yet redirected voice yields genuine institutional responsiveness, I draw on a framework of democratic accountability, understood as the institutional capacity of organized groups to voice claims, contest decisions, and obtain a meaningful response. The Chinese component is a digital ethnography of obesity communities on WeChat, Douyin, and RedNote, with interviews of patients, moderators, company representatives, and foundation staff. The U.S. component assembles a documentary corpus from organizations such as the Obesity Action Coalition and the National Association to Advance Fat Acceptance, alongside FDA docket comments and advisory records on anti-obesity medications. The project extends my account of knowledge brokerage from expert to lay expertise, asking how corporate strategy and state priority together broker patient knowledge into policy.</p>

<h4 class="entry-card__subtitle"><span class="idx">2.</span><em>Cui bono?</em> Who Pays for Anti-Obesity Medication</h4>
<p>In 2024, China launched a three-year national campaign for weight management that emphasized physical activity and a non-pharmaceutical approach, yet that same year approved semaglutide (Wegovy) for chronic weight management while leaving its weight-loss use outside public insurance. The United Kingdom took the opposite path: NICE recommended coverage of Wegovy and Mounjaro, then the NHS constrained access through local commissioning. Both states face the same dilemma of balancing public health needs against the cost of treatment, but they resolve it differently.</p>
<p>Pharmaceuticalization research explains how obesity became a target for drug intervention, yet it draws mainly on Anglo-American cases and stops at clinical adoption, leaving unexamined how states decide whether to fund these treatments. This project asks under what conditions, and through what strategies, governments convert contested knowledge about anti-obesity medicines into binding rules about public payment, and who bears the cost and who benefits. Comparing China and the United Kingdom, it identifies the mechanisms through which the state, expert bodies, and industry shape coverage, and tests them against rival explanations. For China it reuses my 2026 Shanghai and Beijing fieldwork, conference observation, and interviews alongside policy documents and clinical guidelines. For the United Kingdom it relies on NICE technology appraisals, NHS commissioning guidance, and local position statements, complemented by a small number of elite interviews, analyzed through a process-tracing sequence from authoritative evidence to the populations ultimately eligible for public reimbursement.</p>

<h3 class="entry-card__title">Fitness, Health, and Community</h3>
<div class="entry-card__body">
<p>My second research stream examines how people understand and practice fitness, and how fitness becomes embedded in everyday life, social relationships, and institutions. Since 2023 I have developed three related ethnographic studies with university students, gym members, and YMCA members in Greater Boston. Across these projects I examine fitness as a temporal and embodied practice, a site where meanings of health and well-being are negotiated, and a basis for sociality and community-making.</p>
<p><strong>Fitness as a Temporal Craft</strong> draws on carnal sociology and theories of time to conceptualise fitness as a temporal practice through which people sustain bodily routines and biographical continuity amid changing life structures. <strong>Making Sense of Fitness and Health</strong> examines how 18&ndash;30 year-olds define and relate fitness and health, and how their accounts complicate the concept of healthism. <strong>Building Community Through Fitness</strong> draws on interviews with 23 YMCA members, observations at 13 branches in Greater Boston, and archival materials to ask how exercise fosters social ties, belonging, and civic engagement.</p>
<p>This stream has produced a solo chapter in a 2025 Routledge volume on fitness gamification. The three papers are listed on the <a href="{{ '/publications/' | relative_url }}">Publications</a> page.</p>

Taken together, these projects extend biomedicalization and pharmaceuticalization theory beyond the North American and Western European settings in which they were largely developed. They ask not only how a health problem becomes something a state pays to treat, but also how states, firms, and patients decide whose knowledge, claims, and bodies count in that decision.

My research develops along two streams, and a third that grows out of the first.

## Stream 1 — What Became of the Movement? The Politics of Obesity and Weight Control in China

*(Dissertation, Brandeis University)*

My dissertation asks how obesity is governed in contemporary China and how particular forms of knowledge become
authoritative. Around 2024, the Chinese state launched a high-profile response centred on physical activity and
non-pharmacological health maintenance. Yet my fieldwork found its everyday effects more limited than policy documents
suggest, while obesity was increasingly framed as a chronic disease requiring medical treatment, particularly amid the
rapid expansion of GLP-1 therapies. This tension between state-led lifestyle intervention and growing biomedical and
pharmaceutical authority provides the dissertation's central puzzle.

In 2026 I conducted fieldwork in Shanghai and Beijing, combining participant observation with over 50 interviews
across medical, pharmaceutical, fitness, and patient communities, supplemented by policy, media, clinical guideline,
and social-media analysis. My findings show that obesity is governed through competition and coordination among
multiple forms of expertise, with the state actively **brokering** their authority by selecting, endorsing,
standardising, and sometimes resisting particular claims. Non-state actors therefore seek not only professional
legitimacy but also institutional relationships through which their knowledge can become authoritative, while patients
draw on embodied and online knowledge to negotiate expert advice.

## Stream 2 — Fitness, Health, and Community

My second research stream examines how people understand and practice fitness, and how fitness becomes embedded in
everyday life, social relationships, and institutions. Since 2023 I have developed three related ethnographic studies
with university students, gym members, and YMCA members. Across these projects I examine fitness as a temporal and
embodied practice, a site where meanings of health and well-being are negotiated, and a basis for sociality and
community-making.

- **Fitness as a Temporal Craft: A Two-Wave Interview Study of Exercise in Early Adulthood.** Drawing on carnal
  sociology and theories of time, this paper conceptualises fitness as a temporal practice through which people
  sustain bodily routines and biographical continuity amid changing life structures. (*Preparing for submission.*)
- **Making Sense of Fitness and Health: Young Adults' Understandings of Well-Being in Campus Recreation Spaces.**
  Examines how 18–30 year-olds define and relate fitness and health, and how their accounts complicate the concept of
  healthism.
- **Building Community Through Fitness: The YMCA and the Social Life of Exercise.** Based on interviews with 23 YMCA
  members, observations at 13 branches in Greater Boston, and archival materials, this paper asks how exercise fosters
  social ties, belonging, and civic engagement.

This stream has produced a solo chapter in a 2025 Routledge volume on fitness gamification.

## Stream 3 — *Cui bono?* Who Pays for Anti-Obesity Medication

*(Planned postdoctoral research)*

This project compares how China, the United Kingdom, and the United States decide whether — and for whom — to pay for
GLP-1 therapies. It links coverage decisions to prescribing, reimbursement, and expenditure statistics across the
three health systems, and complements them with clinical guidelines, technology appraisals, reimbursement lists, and
interviews with actors who make and contest coverage decisions.

The project develops pharmaceuticalization theory by examining a case it has largely left unexplained: a state that
does not simply resist or retreat from pharmaceutical authority, but actively brokers it. Rather than treating drug
access as the endpoint of pharmaceuticalization, I ask how states determine which patients, therapies, and forms of
expertise merit public payment.

Taken together, these projects extend **biomedicalization and pharmaceuticalization theory** beyond the North American
and Western European settings in which they were largely developed. They ask not only how a health problem becomes
something a state pays to treat, but also how states decide whose health, knowledge, and claims count in that
decision.
